Markets connect buyers and sellers of financial assets. Prices can change as new information, demand and expectations change.

1. What Is the Stock Market?

Key concept

A stock market is a regulated place and network where investors buy and sell shares and other securities. A share represents a small ownership interest in a company. Prices change as buyers and sellers respond to business performance, expectations, news and broader economic conditions. A company’s share price is influenced by expectations about future earnings, interest rates, economic conditions, news and investor sentiment. A rising price does not automatically mean a business is strong, and a falling price does not automatically mean it is cheap. Learn the difference between a market price and the underlying business value.

Example: Example: if a company has 10 million shares and you own 100, you own a very small fraction of that company. Owning a share does not guarantee profit or a dividend.

Practical checklist

Before investing, understand what the company does, how it earns money, its debt, valuation and the risks that could affect its business.

2. Stocks, Indices and Exchanges

Key concept

A stock is a company share. An index tracks a selected group of securities to give a snapshot of part of the market. An exchange provides the regulated trading infrastructure; in India, NSE and BSE are major stock exchanges. Indices use defined selection rules and may be weighted by market value or another method. An index is a benchmark, not a direct guarantee of what every investor earns. Exchanges provide trading systems and rules, while brokers give investors access to those systems; check official exchange and broker information when verifying a security.

Example: An index may rise even when some of its constituent stocks fall, because the overall movement depends on how its constituents are weighted.

Practical checklist

Do not assume that an index represents every company or that an index rising means every investor has made money.

3. Primary and Secondary Markets

Key concept

In the primary market, securities are issued to investors for the first time or through a new issue. In the secondary market, investors trade securities with one another after issuance. IPOs are one example of primary-market activity. New issues allow companies or other issuers to raise capital, subject to applicable rules and disclosures. In secondary markets, buyers and sellers trade existing securities, and the company does not usually receive the proceeds of each exchange trade. IPO applications can be oversubscribed, undersubscribed or list below the issue price, so an IPO is not a guaranteed gain.

Example: When you buy an already-listed share through a broker, you generally trade in the secondary market; your money typically goes to the seller, not directly to the company.

Practical checklist

Read offer documents carefully, understand the use of proceeds and risks, and do not apply to an issue just because it is popular.

4. Market Orders and Limit Orders

Key concept

A market order seeks execution promptly at the best available prices, but the final price may differ from the last displayed price. A limit order specifies the maximum price you will pay to buy or minimum price you will accept to sell; execution is not guaranteed. Market orders prioritise execution, not a particular price; in a fast-moving or thinly traded market, the execution price can be less favourable than expected. Limit orders control the highest buying price or lowest selling price, but may not execute at all. Always understand order validity, quantity and the order status shown by your broker.

Example: If a share is displayed at ₹100, a market buy may execute at a slightly higher price in a fast market. A limit buy at ₹98 will not execute above ₹98.

Practical checklist

Check order type, quantity, price and product settings before confirming. Thinly traded securities can have wider bid–ask spreads.

5. Demat and Trading Accounts

Key concept

A demat account holds securities electronically. A trading account is used to place buy and sell orders through a broker. A bank account is commonly used to move money to and from the broker, subject to the broker and product process. These accounts serve different purposes and may be provided by different regulated entities. Securities are held electronically in a demat account, while the trading account transmits orders to the market. Review account-opening charges, annual maintenance charges, brokerage, statutory charges and the provider’s official terms before opening an account.

Example: Buying a delivery share usually involves a trading order and securities being credited to the demat account after settlement. Intraday products may work differently.

Practical checklist

Use registered brokers and official apps, keep KYC details current, enable account security and never share OTPs or passwords.

6. Price, Volume and Liquidity

Key concept

Price is the level at which a trade occurs; volume measures how many units traded over a period. Liquidity describes how easily an asset can be bought or sold without materially moving its price. An asset with high liquidity generally has many buyers and sellers and a smaller gap between buying and selling prices. Low liquidity can make it harder to exit at a fair price, especially during market stress. Volume alone does not prove that an investment is safe or that a price trend will continue.

Example: A share with low trading volume may show a quote of ₹50 but have few buyers nearby, so a larger order can execute at very different prices.

Practical checklist

Look beyond the displayed price. Check volume, bid–ask spread, order-book depth and whether the instrument suits your needs.

7. Dividends, Splits and Corporate Actions

Key concept

A dividend is a distribution a company may make to shareholders, subject to its decisions and applicable rules. A stock split changes the number of shares and per-share price proportionately in principle; it does not by itself create extra wealth. Corporate actions can change cash flows, the number of shares held or the information investors need to assess a company. A split generally changes the number of shares and per-share price proportionately, while dividends depend on company decisions and eligibility rules. Read official company announcements and understand record dates and other applicable dates.

Example: In a 2-for-1 split, 10 shares may become 20 while the theoretical per-share price halves, leaving the total value broadly unchanged before market movements.

Practical checklist

Read official exchange and company announcements; dates, eligibility and tax treatment can matter.

8. Risk, Return and Diversification

Key concept

Return is the gain or loss on an investment. Risk includes the possibility of loss, volatility, liquidity problems and events that damage an investment thesis. Diversification spreads exposure across different assets or sectors, but cannot eliminate all risk. Higher potential returns are often associated with higher uncertainty, but taking more risk does not guarantee a higher return. Diversification means spreading exposure across companies, sectors or asset types so one holding has less influence on the overall portfolio; it cannot eliminate market-wide losses. Consider goals, time horizon, liquidity needs and ability to bear losses.

Example: Putting all savings into one company creates concentrated risk. Holding a range of suitable investments can reduce dependence on one outcome, though values can still fall together.

Practical checklist

Keep emergency savings separate, match investments to goals and time horizon, and avoid investing money you cannot afford to lose.
