1. Trading vs Investing

Key concept

Trading usually seeks to benefit from shorter-term price movements; investing generally focuses on ownership and long-term goals. Neither approach guarantees returns. They require different amounts of time, knowledge, temperament and risk control. Trading typically involves more frequent decisions and may require close monitoring, while investing often focuses on business or asset fundamentals over a longer horizon. Both can lose money, and active trading can add costs, emotional pressure and execution risk. Decide your purpose, time commitment and loss tolerance before choosing an approach; avoid copying another person’s trades without understanding them.

Example: A trader may plan a position around a defined short-term setup; an investor may evaluate a company’s business and hold through multiple market cycles.

Practical checklist

Choose an approach that fits your time, financial situation and ability to handle losses. Do not trade simply because markets are active.

2. Fundamental Analysis

Key concept

Fundamental analysis studies a business and its financial position, including revenue, profit, cash flow, debt, competitive position and management. Valuation compares the price with estimates of business value; a good company can still be overpriced. Review more than a single headline or one quarter’s results. Revenue shows sales, profit reflects earnings under accounting rules, and cash flow helps assess how money actually moves through the business. Debt, margins, competition, governance and valuation all matter; financial statements and forecasts have limitations, and a sound business can still be a poor investment at an excessive price.

Example: Two companies may report similar profits but have different debt, growth prospects and valuations, so their investment risks may differ.

Practical checklist

Use audited reports and official filings. Treat forecasts as uncertain, compare multiple years and understand that past performance does not ensure future results.

3. Technical Charts and Trends

Key concept

Charts display historical prices and sometimes volume. Candlesticks summarise open, high, low and close prices for a period. Support, resistance and trend lines are tools traders use to organise observations, not reliable predictions by themselves. Candlesticks summarise open, high, low and close prices over a chosen period; volume can help show how much activity accompanied a move. Support and resistance are areas where price previously reacted, not guaranteed barriers. Indicators are derived from historical data and can lag or give false signals, so test a complete method and include fees and slippage.

Example: A breakout above a previous range can fail and reverse. A chart pattern is a hypothesis to test, not proof that a price will move in a particular direction.

Practical checklist

Use a consistent method, test it on adequate data, account for costs and avoid relying on a single indicator.

4. Risk–Reward and Position Sizing

Key concept

Risk management sets limits before a trade or investment. Position sizing determines how much exposure to take, while a stop order may help manage exits but can execute at a worse price during gaps or fast markets. Risk per position should be set before entry and considered alongside the total exposure across all open positions. A risk–reward ratio compares a planned potential loss with a planned potential gain, but neither outcome is certain. Stop orders may execute at a different price in a gap or fast market, so position size and the possibility of losing more than expected must be considered.

Example: If a planned trade has a possible loss of ₹500 at the chosen exit level, position size should be based on that amount and your overall risk limit—not on the profit you hope to make.

Practical checklist

Never risk money needed for essentials. Include fees, taxes, slippage and the possibility that an exit order will not fill as expected.

5. Trading Psychology and Discipline

Key concept

Fear, greed, impatience and the urge to recover losses can lead to impulsive decisions. A written plan helps define entry criteria, invalidation, risk limit and reasons to exit before emotions take over. A written plan can reduce impulsive choices by defining the setup, entry reason, exit conditions, maximum risk and review process in advance. A win does not necessarily mean a good decision, and a loss does not automatically mean a bad one; evaluate whether you followed a sound process. Avoid revenge trading, excessive leverage and decisions made while stressed or trying to recover money quickly.

Example: After a losing trade, increasing size just to win the money back is revenge trading and can magnify losses.

Practical checklist

Keep a journal, review decisions rather than only outcomes, take breaks when emotional and do not borrow to speculate.

6. Long-Term Investing and Compounding

Key concept

Compounding occurs when returns remain invested and future returns can be earned on both the original amount and earlier returns. Actual returns vary and may be negative; compounding is not a promise of a fixed outcome. Compounding means returns left invested may contribute to future returns, so time and consistency can matter. Real market returns fluctuate and may be negative for extended periods; fees, taxes, missed contributions and withdrawals affect results. Compounding examples are illustrations rather than promises, and no investment return should be assumed to be fixed or guaranteed.

Example: Regular contributions can build a portfolio over time, but the final value depends on contribution amount, duration, costs and market performance.

Practical checklist

Define the goal and time horizon, diversify appropriately, review costs and avoid reacting to every short-term market move.

7. Mutual Funds and SIPs

Key concept

A mutual fund pools money from investors and invests according to its scheme mandate. A Systematic Investment Plan (SIP) is a way to invest a chosen amount periodically; it is not a separate product and does not guarantee profit. A mutual fund pools investors’ money and follows a stated investment mandate. An SIP is a periodic contribution method, not a guarantee of profit or a separate type of fund. Understand the scheme’s objective, riskometer, holdings, expense ratio, exit load and time horizon; past performance may not continue and a diversified fund can still fall in value.

Example: A monthly SIP buys units at different prices over time. This can spread purchase timing, but it cannot prevent losses if the investments decline.

Practical checklist

Read the scheme information document, riskometer, expense ratio, exit load and portfolio. Select a scheme based on suitability, not past returns alone.

8. Trading Costs, Taxes and Records

Key concept

Trading results are affected by brokerage, exchange and statutory charges, taxes, spreads and slippage. Tax treatment depends on instrument, holding period, transaction type and current law. Gross profit or loss does not show the full result after brokerage, exchange charges, taxes, spreads and slippage. Rules and tax treatment can change and may depend on instrument type, holding period and personal circumstances. Keep contract notes, statements and transaction records, and consult a qualified tax professional for advice on your own situation.

Example: A strategy showing a small gross gain may become unprofitable after repeated transaction costs and taxes.

Practical checklist

Review the broker’s contract notes and statements, maintain records and consult a qualified tax professional for personal tax advice.
